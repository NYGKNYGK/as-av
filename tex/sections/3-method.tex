\subsection{SPC model uncertainty}
\label{subsec:spc-model-uncertainty}

This section quantifies the height uncertainty remaining after SPC reconstruction and predicts how a new acquisition could reduce it. We briefly summarize the reconstruction model needed for this analysis, then develop the joint height covariance and the criterion for selecting a useful viewing direction and rotation phase. Figure~\ref{fig:spc-model-uncertainty} distinguishes the established SPC reconstruction in blue~\citep{gaskell2023,palmer2022} from the added statistical analysis in purple. Camera calibration, camera poses, Sun directions, lateral maplet registration, and image-to-surface associations are held fixed; the resulting height uncertainty is conditional on these assumptions.

\noindent\textbf{SPC reconstruction background.}
SPC represents a local surface patch by a \emph{maplet}, with heights $h(x,y)$, slopes $t_x=\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}$ and $t_y=\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}$, and relative albedo $A$. Heights and horizontal coordinates are expressed in meters in fixed nominal maplet frames, retaining uncertainty in vertical anchoring. The slopes determine the unit normal $\mathbf N$:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf N=\frac{(-t_x,-t_y,1)^T}{\sqrt{1+t_x^2+t_y^2}}
\end{equation}

For image $k$, the normal, Sun direction, and viewing direction define the incidence angle $\alpha_k$, emission angle $\beta_k$, and phase angle $\gamma_k$. The predicted brightness is

\begin{equation}
    I_{p,k}=\lambda_k A \Phi(\cos\alpha_k,\cos\beta_k,\gamma_k)
\end{equation}

where $\lambda_k$ normalizes image brightness and $\Phi$ is the reflectance function. Using valid, associated image samples, SPC iteratively fits slopes and albedo to weighted brightness residuals. Following its notation, $A=1+t_z$, where $t_z$ is an albedo parameter, not a third slope. Relative albedo is given a fixed mean to remove its common scale ambiguity with $\lambda_k$.

The fitted slopes are integrated into a consistent height field using neighboring height differences and reference constraints from stereo landmarks, limbs, overlapping maplets, or independent terrain data. Slopes are then recomputed from this field and albedo is refitted~\citep{gaskell2023,palmer2022}. Two features of this process motivate the uncertainty analysis: terrain tilt and albedo can explain similar brightness changes, while integration couples heights through shared slope and reference errors. Complementary geometry helps separate the former~\citep{palmer2024geometry}; the latter requires retaining spatial covariance. The reconstructed heights provide a point estimate, whose remaining uncertainty cannot be inferred from brightness residuals alone.

\noindent\textbf{A statistical model for height uncertainty.}
The uncertainty analysis describes which height variations remain compatible with the observations and how those variations are coupled. Let $\boldsymbol\xi=(\mathbf h^T,\boldsymbol\eta^T)^T$, where $\mathbf h$ contains the $N$ heights and $\boldsymbol\eta$ contains albedos, image normalization coefficients, and uncertain shared control offsets. Let $\mathbf z$ collect brightness measurements, image-space stereo or limb observations, and independent control data, with predictions $\mathbf f(\boldsymbol\xi)$. For zero-mean Gaussian errors of locally fixed covariance $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ and a Gaussian prior with mean $\boldsymbol\xi_0$ and precision $\boldsymbol\Lambda_0$, define the joint maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate by
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \hat{\boldsymbol\xi}&=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol\xi} Q(\boldsymbol\xi),\\
        Q(\boldsymbol\xi)&=\tfrac12\|\mathbf z-\mathbf f(\boldsymbol\xi)\|^2_{\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}}\\
        &\quad+\tfrac12\|\boldsymbol\xi-\boldsymbol\xi_0\|^2_{\boldsymbol\Lambda_0},
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:uncertainty-joint-map}
\end{equation}
where $\|\mathbf v\|^2_{\mathbf W}=\mathbf v^T\mathbf W\mathbf v$. This is an additional joint statistical model, rather than a restatement of the alternating SPC updates. Joint probabilistic formulations are also used in PhoMo~\citep{driver2025}, although its state and reconstruction procedure differ from the maplet model here.

Convergence of the SPC reconstruction does not establish that it minimizes $Q$. Its output can initialize a joint refinement under Equation~\ref{eq:uncertainty-joint-map}. A posterior interpretation of the covariance below requires refinement to a local minimum of $Q$, or verification that the SPC solution already meets that criterion. Without this consistency, the inverse information is a planning proxy; uncertainty of the actual SPC estimator must instead be assessed by propagating perturbations through the reconstruction or by repeated synthetic reconstructions.

At a consistent fitted state, the local measurement model is
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf J&=
        \left.\frac{\partial\mathbf f}{\partial\boldsymbol\xi}
        \right|_{\hat{\boldsymbol\xi}},\\
        \delta\mathbf z&\approx\mathbf J\,\delta\boldsymbol\xi+\boldsymbol\epsilon,
        \quad \boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf0,\boldsymbol\Sigma).
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:uncertainty-jacobian}
\end{equation}
Height derivatives must include the affected slopes, normals, and geometric predictions, including image projection where appropriate. Fixed associations identify corresponding measurements; they do not freeze the predictions when heights change. Under a local Gaussian approximation, the Gauss--Newton precision and joint posterior covariance are
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf F=\mathbf J^T\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf J
                 +\boldsymbol\Lambda_0,
    \qquad
    \mathbf C\approx\mathbf F^{-1}.
    \label{eq:uncertainty-information}
\end{equation}
The Jacobian describes how strongly each parameter change affects the observations, while $\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}$ weights that response by measurement precision. A height variation that barely changes the predicted data relative to their noise is weakly constrained by the measurements and retains large uncertainty unless constrained by the prior. This approximation neglects higher-order effects of the nonlinear observation model. Coordinate and albedo-scale conventions are fixed first; remaining physical modes, including ambiguities involving image normalization, must be constrained by observations or justified priors. Numerical damping supplies no information, and algorithmic integration or photometric weights are not automatically calibrated precisions.

Marginalizing the auxiliary parameters gives
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf C_{hh}&=[\mathbf C]_{1:N,\,1:N}\\
        &\approx(\mathbf F_{hh}-\mathbf F_{h\eta}
          \mathbf F_{\eta\eta}^{-1}\mathbf F_{\eta h})^{-1}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:uncertainty-height-covariance}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, invert the joint information matrix and extract its height block. Using only $\mathbf F_{hh}^{-1}$ would hold albedo and the other auxiliary quantities perfectly known. The pointwise height uncertainty is
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{h_i}=\sqrt{(\mathbf C_{hh})_{ii}}.
    \label{eq:uncertainty-pointwise-height}
\end{equation}
The covariance describes posterior spread conditional on the adopted model and fixed geometry, rather than the repeated-sampling variance of an arbitrary SPC estimator. A small $\sigma_{h_i}$ therefore indicates strong modeled constraints, not demonstrated accuracy relative to the true surface.

Off-diagonal entries retain shared errors. For example, let $\delta h_i=b+\nu_i$ and $\delta h_j=b+\nu_j$, where the shared vertical-reference offset $b$ is independent of the remaining errors. Then
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Cov}(\delta h_i,\delta h_j)
    =\operatorname{Var}(b)+\operatorname{Cov}(\nu_i,\nu_j).
    \label{eq:uncertainty-shared-reference}
\end{equation}
The common offset cancels from $h_i-h_j$, while both heights remain uncertain relative to the fixed nominal reference. This is the example in Figure~\ref{fig:spc-model-uncertainty}(d). If maplet position or orientation is also uncertain, it must be included in the state and propagated through the body-frame point transformation before claiming full spatial uncertainty.

The observation model must also retain dependence introduced by reused data. Image-derived slopes and overlapping maplets are not independent copies of their source observations. Neither landmark formal uncertainties nor the empirical spread of overlapping-maplet heights supplies the full $\mathbf C_{hh}$ defined here~\citep{gaskell2023,weirich2022}.

\noindent\textbf{Information supplied by a new acquisition.}
The covariance identifies the surface variations that remain uncertain; selecting a useful observation requires predicting how a new image would respond to those variations. An acquisition $a$ specifies a rotation phase and camera pose. Camera position determines viewing directions, visibility, geometric parallax, and projected pixel scale; rotation phase changes the illumination relative to the body and hence the photometric sensitivity. The current surface is used to identify samples that would be illuminated, visible, and resolved. At each candidate geometry and a fixed visibility mask, linearize the new observation model as
\begin{equation}
    \delta\mathbf z_a\approx
        \mathbf B_a\delta\boldsymbol\xi+
        \mathbf H_a\delta\mathbf q_a+\boldsymbol\epsilon_a,
    \label{eq:uncertainty-new-measurement}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf B_a$ differentiates with respect to the existing state, $\mathbf q_a$ contains new nuisance parameters such as $\lambda_a$, and $\mathbf H_a$ differentiates with respect to them. Derivatives and image noise are forecast at a specified nominal exposure and normalization. Let $\boldsymbol\epsilon_a\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf0,\mathbf N_a)$ be independent of previous errors conditional on the shared state. Assume an independent prior precision $\boldsymbol\Lambda_{q_a}$ for the new parameters; zero precision is allowed if the new measurements constrain the eliminated block. Shared uncertain calibration or control parameters belong in $\boldsymbol\xi$, rather than being introduced as independent errors for each image.

Eliminating $\mathbf q_a$ from the augmented information matrix yields
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf D_a&=\mathbf H_a^T\mathbf N_a^{-1}\mathbf H_a
                       +\boldsymbol\Lambda_{q_a},\\
        \mathbf U_a&=\mathbf B_a^T\mathbf N_a^{-1}\mathbf H_a,\\
        \Delta\mathbf F_a&=\mathbf B_a^T\mathbf N_a^{-1}\mathbf B_a
                       -\mathbf U_a\mathbf D_a^{-1}\mathbf U_a^T.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:uncertainty-effective-information}
\end{equation}
This requires $\mathbf D_a$ to be invertible on the retained nuisance subspace. The subtraction removes apparent information that can be explained by adjusting the new image parameters. When there are no new unknowns, it reduces to the usual Jacobian information update. It also avoids trying to invert an old-state information matrix augmented by an entirely unconstrained new parameter.

The updated covariance of the existing state and the pointwise variance gains are
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf C^+(a)&\approx
        (\mathbf F+\Delta\mathbf F_a)^{-1},\\
        \Delta\sigma_{h_i}^2(a)&=
        \mathbf e_i^T[\mathbf C-\mathbf C^+(a)]\mathbf e_i .
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:uncertainty-observation-gain}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf e_i$ selects height $i$ from the existing joint state. At this fixed linearization, $\Delta\mathbf F_a$ is positive semidefinite and the predicted variance gains are nonnegative. Future pixel values are unnecessary for this local forecast, but the Jacobians and noise model depend on the current estimate. The forecast does not average over uncertain terrain, visibility changes, or future nonlinear fits.

A useful viewing direction produces sensitivity to parameter combinations that remain uncertain under $\mathbf C$. A region with large $\sigma_{h_i}$ may yield little gain if the proposed image cannot distinguish its height changes from albedo or normalization changes. Conversely, complementary geometry can separate these effects, and constraining a shared mode can improve several heights together. Repeated geometry can still reduce independent random noise, although it may do little to resolve a weakly observed direction. Thus, the choice depends jointly on the current covariance, the candidate viewing and lighting geometry, and measurement precision.

\noindent\textbf{Acquisition objective and reconstruction feedback.}
Define the ideal next acquisition by
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        G(a)&=\sum_i\omega_i\,\Delta\sigma_{h_i}^2(a),\\
        a^\star&=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal A}G(a).
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:uncertainty-next-view}
\end{equation}
Here $\omega_i\geq0$ is the represented surface-area weight, normalized to $\sum_i\omega_i=1$, and $\mathcal A$ is the feasible acquisition set. The weights prevent dense tessellation from receiving extra priority. Maximizing $G$ is equivalent to minimizing the area-weighted trace of the future marginal height covariance: a weighted A-optimal design criterion~\citep{huan2024design}. This standard statistical criterion defines the planning target; its application here depends on the specified SPC observation and uncertainty model.

The PointNet++ planner uses this criterion as a statistical reference; Section~\ref{subsec:observation-planner} distinguishes its single-view and sequence formulations from the empirical training objective. It predicts phase--pose pairs from geometry, marginal height uncertainty, and observing context, returning the first accepted pair as the next proposal. Because identical marginal variances can conceal different correlations, these inputs do not determine the exact gain for every statistical state. Both the learned policy and a critic using only these inputs share this limitation.

After observing, rerun reconstruction, update the statistical fit or validate its consistency, and recompute uncertainty at corresponding surface locations. Compare gains using the same area weights, reference conventions, prior, and noise-model calibration. Actual gains after nonlinear refitting can be negative; the nonnegative forecast applies only to the fixed local model. Several acquisitions must be evaluated jointly, since summing their separate gains from the same original state would count redundant information more than once.

Finally, modeled uncertainty must be checked against geometric accuracy. A wrong reflectance model or unresolved terrain can produce systematic height error despite small conditional covariance~\citep{palmer2024accuracy}. Independent terrain measurements or repeated reconstructions with known synthetic truth are required to assess error and uncertainty calibration~\citep{weirich2022,alasad2021}. Thus, covariance reduction is a model-based planning objective, while final surface accuracy remains a separate validation outcome.

\subsection{Observation planner}
\label{subsec:observation-planner}

Section~\ref{subsec:spc-model-uncertainty} defines a statistical reference for uncertainty reduction. We now describe a planner that maps the current surface, marginal height uncertainties, and observing conditions to $K$ chronologically ordered rotation phases and camera positions. The camera attitude follows a body-centered pointing rule. For $K=1$, the reference criterion is the single-acquisition gain in Equation~\ref{eq:uncertainty-next-view}; for $K>1$, it is the joint gain of a finite observation sequence. Training uses realized reconstruction gains as empirical supervision, as defined below. Executing the first accepted acquisition and replanning is a receding-horizon heuristic; its first acquisition need not maximize the single-view criterion. Here $K$ is a fixed proposal budget for each network configuration, and both phases and positions are continuous outputs.

The reference architecture combines a PointNet++ surface encoder~\citep{qi2017pointnetpp}, variance-weighted feature pooling, an analytic rotation layer, and coupled phase and position heads. These components specify how SPC uncertainty enters the decision; their numerical settings are not an experimentally established optimum. Learned view selection from point clouds, continuous camera actions, and illumination-aware small-body scheduling have precedents in PC-NBV, GenNBV, and the work of \citet{piccinin2022}, respectively~\citep{zeng2020,chen2024}. The distinction pursued here is their combination with an SPC height-variance objective and continuous joint prediction of observation time and position.

Figure~\ref{fig:planner-architecture} follows the information flow: encode the uncertain surface, fuse observing context, predict phases, condition surface features on their illumination, and predict positions. Pose conversion and geometric checks then produce a proposed observation sequence.

\subsubsection{Planning objective and SPC inputs}
\label{subsubsec:planner-input}

For a geometrically accepted sequence $\mathcal Q=(a_1,\ldots,a_L)$, $L\leq K$, define the reference objective
\begin{equation}
    G(\mathcal Q)=\sum_i\omega_i
       \left[(\mathbf C_{hh})_{ii}
              -(\mathbf C_{hh}^{+}(\mathcal Q))_{ii}\right].
    \label{eq:planner-sequence-gain}
\end{equation}
The posterior is forecast jointly from all new measurements and nuisance parameters under the fixed local model of Section~\ref{subsec:spc-model-uncertainty}. The evaluation locations and area weights remain fixed across candidates. This is a sequence version of the weighted A-optimal criterion, not a sum of independently computed gains. An empty sequence has zero gain. This local forecast supplies a design reference; the empirical training objective uses realized reduction after reconstruction and may rank sequences differently.

After the SPC fitting and height integration have converged, the interface receives registered surface points $\mathbf X_i^B$, their height standard deviations $\sigma_{h_i}$ from Equation~\ref{eq:uncertainty-pointwise-height}, and their surface associations. The frame $B$ has its origin at the rotation center and its $z$ axis along the known spin axis. Maplet coordinates and normals are transformed into this common frame; the uncertainty remains attached to the same estimated height and physical surface location. It is supplied by the additional statistical model in Section~\ref{subsec:spc-model-uncertainty}, subject to its estimator-consistency and calibration requirements, rather than inferred from brightness residual magnitude or image count. Normals $\mathbf n_i^B$, albedo, and represented surface area are retained as auxiliary surface attributes.

Overlapping maplets are associated before sampling, so repeated estimates of one region do not count as additional surface area. We select $M=2048$ existing height samples by spatial stratification with allocation proportional to represented area. Each sample retains its own uncertainty and a normalized represented-area weight $\omega_i$, with $\sum_i\omega_i=1$, following the weighting principle in Equation~\ref{eq:uncertainty-next-view}. Averaging several standard deviations would not give the uncertainty of an averaged height, because the latter also depends on covariance; this interface therefore retains actual height estimates and their marginal uncertainties. Invalid or missing uncertainties are masked rather than assigned zero. If fewer than $M$ valid samples exist, padding is excluded from grouping and pooling; the planner reports insufficient input if none exist.

We normalize by a fixed enclosing radius $S$ for the current body model,
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\mathbf X}_i=\mathbf X_i^B/S,
    \qquad u_i=\sigma_{h_i}/S.
    \label{eq:planner-input-normalization}
\end{equation}
The input is a coordinate array $\mathbf P_0\in\mathbb R^{M\times3}$ and a feature array $\mathbf F_0\in\mathbb R^{M\times1}$, whose rows are $\bar{\mathbf X}_i$ and $u_i$. A large $u_i$ means that the height remains weakly constrained under the SPC model; it is not a probability that the point is wrong. The uncertainty channel is not independently rescaled to $[0,1]$ each round, which would erase the absolute reduction in uncertainty as reconstruction progresses. The scale $S$ is retained for decoding and supplied relative to a fixed reference length. The same geometry with a different uncertainty map is consequently a different planner input.

The four-channel representation in Figure~\ref{fig:method-framework} contains coordinates and marginal height uncertainties. The full joint covariance remains in the SPC statistical state. Consequently, this interface does not uniquely determine $G$: states with identical geometry and marginal variances can differ in height correlations, height--albedo coupling, or image-normalization uncertainty and favor different acquisitions. The planner learns a decision from the states represented in training; it is not an exact information-gain optimizer for every SPC state. Additional joint-information summaries would be needed to distinguish these cases.

\subsubsection{Uncertainty-aware surface encoding}
\label{subsubsec:planner-encoder}

The encoder follows PointNet++ set abstraction: sample representative centers, group spatial neighbors, apply a shared pointwise multilayer perceptron (MLP), and pool within each group~\citep{qi2017pointnetpp}. This extends the symmetric point-set aggregation of PointNet~\citep{qi2017pointnet} to local and regional surface structure. For center $j$ at level $\ell$, the grouped feature is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf f_j^{(\ell+1)}=\max_{i\in\mathcal N_j^{(\ell)}}
    \psi_\ell\!\left(
    [\bar{\mathbf X}_i^{(\ell)}-\bar{\mathbf X}_j^{(\ell)},
      \mathbf f_i^{(\ell)}]\right),
    \label{eq:planner-set-abstraction}
\end{equation}
where the maximum is channel-wise, $\psi_\ell$ shares weights across neighbors, and $\mathbf f_i^{(0)}=u_i$. Farthest-point sampling selects centers on the surface; a radius query constructs each neighborhood, retaining the nearest points if its capacity is exceeded. Unused entries are masked before pooling. A coordinate-based initial center and deterministic tie handling make sampling independent of input ordering apart from geometrically indistinguishable ties. Sampling here compresses the reconstructed surface and does not discretize the camera action space.

The shared MLP combines relative position with uncertainty, distinguishing geometrically similar regions with different constraint strengths. Regional aggregation adds surrounding terrain, while global pooling summarizes the body. These features describe where uncertainty occurs; viewing and illumination geometry are still needed to predict whether an acquisition can reduce it.

Table~\ref{tab:planner-architecture} gives the reference configuration. Coordinates are carried separately from learned features and concatenated as in Equation~\ref{eq:planner-set-abstraction}. The first two stages capture local uncertainty patterns and their regional context. The final stage groups all remaining centers, concatenates their body-frame coordinates and features, and produces a global descriptor $\mathbf g\in\mathbb R^{1024}$. Pointwise affine layers use batch normalization and ReLU; inference uses the stored normalization statistics. The descriptor is recomputed when uncertainty changes, even if the geometry and neighborhood indices remain unchanged.

We also retain an explicit summary weighted by unresolved height variance. For each input sample, let $j(i)$ be its nearest valid regional center and form
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf z_i=\chi\!\left(
       [\mathbf f_{j(i)}^{(2)},\bar{\mathbf X}_i,
        \mathbf n_i^B,u_i]\right)\in\mathbb R^{256},
    \label{eq:planner-surface-descriptor}
\end{equation}
where $\chi$ is a shared $263\to256$ affine projection followed by ReLU. The 263 input channels comprise 256 regional features, three coordinates, three normal components, and one uncertainty. Including the sample position and normal gives this branch explicit location and orientation information. Define
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        w_i^U&=\omega_i u_i^2,\qquad U_0=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_i^U,\\
        \mathbf g_U&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_i^U\mathbf z_i}
                              {U_0+\varepsilon}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:planner-uncertainty-pooling}
\end{equation}
Here $\varepsilon>0$ protects the denominator. The descriptors $\mathbf g$ and $\mathbf g_U$ retain overall shape and emphasize uncertain terrain, respectively; $U_0$ preserves the uncertainty magnitude removed by normalizing the weighted mean. Area weighting reduces sensitivity to sampling density in this branch, although PointNet++ neighborhood pooling is not itself an area integral. The variance weights are a design heuristic motivated by the objective, not an estimate of acquisition gain: a large variance may belong to a mode that the proposed image cannot constrain.

\subsubsection{Rotation and illumination conditioning}
\label{subsubsec:planner-rotation}

Unlike a static-object feature extractor, the planner must distinguish the same terrain observed at different rotation phases. Assume rigid rotation about the known axis at positive angular speed $\omega$, fixed directional illumination, and exposures short enough to neglect rotational image smear. With current time $t_0$ and phase $\phi_0$,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \phi(t)&=\phi_0+\omega(t-t_0),\\
        \mathbf R_{IB}(\phi)&=\mathbf R_{IB}(0)\mathbf R_z(\phi),\\
        \mathbf s^B(\phi)&=\mathbf R_{IB}(\phi)^T\mathbf s^I.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:planner-rotating-light}
\end{equation}
The matrix $\mathbf R_{IB}$ maps body to inertial coordinates, and $\mathbf s^I$ is the fixed unit direction toward the Sun. The mesh, its normals, and its height uncertainty remain in the body frame. We rotate the illumination and viewing directions into that frame, rather than rotate and re-encode the entire mesh for each possible observation. Rotation alone does not decrease $\sigma_{h_i}$: it changes the measurements that could be obtained next.

For an inertial camera center $\mathbf p^I$ and rotation center $\mathbf o^I$, its equivalent body-frame position is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf p^B(\phi)
      =\mathbf R_{IB}(\phi)^T(\mathbf p^I-\mathbf o^I).
    \label{eq:planner-fixed-camera}
\end{equation}
Even at a fixed inertial camera position relative to the center, rotation changes local viewing and illumination directions and can add stereo or photometric constraints. Predicting time therefore complements predicting position. A variant restricted to a prescribed camera path must include that path in the observing context and obtain positions from it, rather than decode independent centers; Equation~\ref{eq:planner-fixed-camera} gives the fixed-position case.

The connection to SPC is through its brightness derivatives. Let $\mathbf S_i(\phi)$ be $\mathbf s^B(\phi)$ transformed into point $i$'s maplet axes. For the near-horizontal Lambertian example, using $A_i=1+t_{z,i}$ as in Section~\ref{subsec:spc-model-uncertainty},
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial I_{p,i}}{\partial(t_{x,i},t_{y,i},t_{z,i})}
    \approx\lambda(-A_i S_{i,x},-A_i S_{i,y},S_{i,z}).
    \label{eq:planner-rotation-sensitivity}
\end{equation}
Changing phase changes these sensitivity rows. When they add independent constraints after nuisance-parameter elimination, they can reduce the slope--albedo ambiguity. This motivates exploiting rotation rather than maximizing brightness. The geometry tests of \citet{palmer2024geometry} support complementary illumination and viewing for SPC, but do not establish that every fixed-Sun rotation sequence provides sufficient diversity.

The incidence cosine can be evaluated cheaply for every sampled normal:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_i^{\mathrm{sun}}(\phi)
      =c_{i,c}\cos\phi+c_{i,s}\sin\phi+c_{i,0}.
    \label{eq:planner-incidence-harmonic}
\end{equation}
For $\mathbf n_i^B=(n_x,n_y,n_z)^T$ and $\mathbf s^B(0)=(s_x,s_y,s_z)^T$, the coefficients are $c_{i,c}=n_xs_x+n_ys_y$, $c_{i,s}=n_xs_y-n_ys_x$, and $c_{i,0}=n_zs_z$. They are cached with the geometry and Sun direction. The labels $c_{i,\cdot}$ distinguish these coefficients from the surface albedo $A_i$. This analytic layer supplies the network with the physical effect of phase instead of requiring it to learn a rotation law from examples.

Rotation has specific limits. If the Sun lies along the spin axis, $\mathbf s^B$ does not change. If it is perpendicular to the spin axis, all rotated light directions lie in a plane. In the Lambertian model, even known image normalizations then yield a lighting matrix of rank at most two for the unknown vector $A_i\mathbf N_i$; rotation alone cannot identify all three components. Stereo constraints, justified priors, or a change in solar aspect are needed to resolve the remaining ambiguity. More generally, rank must be assessed using the usable observations and their nuisance parameters.

A facet with $c_{i,0}+\sqrt{c_{i,c}^2+c_{i,s}^2}\leq0$ never receives positive direct incidence. For a fixed inertial camera position relative to the center, the center-to-camera--Sun angle is also invariant: waiting changes local incidence, emission, visibility, and parallax, but cannot supply arbitrary global photometric phase angles. Shadow and visibility tests further restrict which phases are useful.

\subsubsection{Continuous phase and position prediction}
\label{subsubsec:planner-decoder}

Known observing conditions form a context vector $\mathbf e$: the current phase encoded by its sine and cosine, $\mathbf s^B(0)$, spin rate, normalized camera intrinsics, body scale, and acquisition bounds. Exposure, reflectance, and noise-model settings are fixed for a configuration or supplied as additional context when they vary. For planning duration $T$ and minimum separation $\delta_t$, define $\Omega=\omega T$ and $\delta_\phi=\omega\delta_t$, with $\Omega>K\delta_\phi$. We apply the same minimum delay from $t_0$ to the first observation. The body frame is preserved because its orientation relative to the spin axis and Sun has physical meaning.

First concatenate $\mathbf c=[\mathbf g,\mathbf g_U,U_0,\mathbf e]$, of dimension $d_c=1281+\dim\mathbf e$. A phase head with fully connected widths $d_c\to512\to256\to K$ predicts logits $b_1,\ldots,b_K$. Hidden layers use ReLU and the final layer is linear. Chronological phases are obtained by
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        (\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_{K+1})&=\operatorname{softmax}(b_1,\ldots,b_K,0),\\
        \phi_k&=\phi_0+k\delta_\phi
          +(\Omega-K\delta_\phi)\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi_j.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:planner-phase-sequence}
\end{equation}
The final weight reserves unused time after the last observation. These unwrapped phases satisfy $\phi_0<\phi_1<\cdots<\phi_K<\phi_0+\Omega$ and enforce the minimum separation. Acquisition times are $t_k=t_0+(\phi_k-\phi_0)/\omega$. Sine and cosine describe periodic illumination, while the unwrapped increments retain which rotation and time an acquisition belongs to. This avoids treating two phases separated by a full revolution as the same scheduled observation.

Next, evaluate Equation~\ref{eq:planner-incidence-harmonic} at all $K$ predicted phases and construct a smooth illumination gate,
\begin{equation}
    \ell_{ik}=\operatorname{sigmoid}
      \left(\frac{\mu_i^{\mathrm{sun}}(\phi_k)-\mu_{\min}}{\tau_\mu}\right).
    \label{eq:planner-light-gate}
\end{equation}
Here $\mu_{\min}\geq0$ expresses the chosen incidence-quality threshold and $\tau_\mu>0$ controls the transition width. This gate encodes direct incidence, with exact shadow tests performed after position decoding. It is a smooth geometric prior, not a probability of reconstruction success. For each phase, compute
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        W_k&=\sum_i w_i^U\ell_{ik},\\
        \mathbf g_{L,k}&=\frac{\sum_i w_i^U\ell_{ik}\mathbf z_i}
                                   {W_k+\varepsilon},\\
        m_k&=\frac{W_k}{U_0+\varepsilon}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:planner-phase-conditioned-pooling}
\end{equation}
The descriptor $\mathbf g_{L,k}$ emphasizes uncertain terrain with favorable direct incidence; $m_k$ records the weighted amount. The retained descriptors $\mathbf g_U$ and $\mathbf g$ preserve the rest of the surface context. This gate is smooth in phase, allowing gradients through the position head, but neither accounts for cast shadows nor guarantees photometric observability.

For each slot form the 260-dimensional vector
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf q_k=
    [\mathbf g_{L,k},m_k,\sin\phi_k,\cos\phi_k,
        (\phi_k-\phi_0)/\Omega].
    \label{eq:planner-phase-token}
\end{equation}
The position head receives $[\mathbf c,\mathbf q_1,\ldots,\mathbf q_K]$ and uses fully connected widths $(d_c+260K)\to512\to256\to4K$. Its linear output is reshaped into $(\mathbf d_k,\zeta_k)$ for every slot, with $\mathbf d_k\in\mathbb R^3$. The head sees the lighting-conditioned descriptors of the entire proposed sequence, allowing positions to be coordinated across phases. The phase head and position head are evaluated within one feed-forward planner call: all phases are produced together, followed by all positions together. There is no reconstruction or greedy view selection between slots.

For normalized distance bounds $1<r_{\min}<r_{\max}$, convert the outputs to body-frame camera centers:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \hat{\mathbf d}_k&=\mathbf d_k/\|\mathbf d_k\|,\\
        r_k&=r_{\min}+(r_{\max}-r_{\min})\operatorname{sigmoid}(\zeta_k),\\
        \mathbf p_k^B&=S r_k\hat{\mathbf d}_k.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:planner-camera-position}
\end{equation}
Direction vectors below a numerical norm tolerance are invalid. The enclosing sphere places camera centers outside the current body; it does not enforce orbital accessibility or a collision-free transfer. The planning objective here concerns reconstruction geometry, without a fuel penalty.

The position head thus receives pooled location, orientation, and terrain features conditioned on predicted illumination. This provides a route from uncertainty to a camera proposal, without preserving every individual surface feature or guaranteeing that the camera sees the emphasized region. Visibility is checked explicitly; information gain and complementarity must be assessed from joint sequence outcomes.

For body-centered imaging, camera orientation is determined by pointing the optical axis toward the body origin, with a fixed roll convention. Let $\mathbf z_k=-\hat{\mathbf d}_k$ be the camera forward axis and let $\mathbf a$ be the body $z$ axis, replaced by the body $y$ axis when nearly parallel to $\mathbf z_k$. Define $\mathbf x_k=\operatorname{normalize}(\mathbf z_k\times\mathbf a)$ and $\mathbf y_k=\mathbf z_k\times\mathbf x_k$. The body-to-camera rotation has these vectors as rows,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf R_{CB,k}=\begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf x_k^T\\ \mathbf y_k^T\\ \mathbf z_k^T
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad \mathbf t_{CB,k}=-\mathbf R_{CB,k}\mathbf p_k^B.
    \label{eq:planner-body-extrinsics}
\end{equation}
This convention uses a forward $z$ axis and downward image $y$ axis. If $\mathbf o^I$ is the inertial position of the rotation center, the corresponding inertial quantities are
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf p_k^I&=\mathbf o^I+\mathbf R_{IB}(\phi_k)\mathbf p_k^B,\\
        \mathbf R_{CI,k}&=\mathbf R_{CB,k}\mathbf R_{IB}(\phi_k)^T,\\
        \mathbf t_{CI,k}&=-\mathbf R_{CI,k}\mathbf p_k^I.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:planner-inertial-extrinsics}
\end{equation}
Rendering the stationary body mesh uses $(\mathbf R_{CB,k},\mathbf t_{CB,k})$ and $\mathbf s^B(\phi_k)$ together. Rendering a rotated inertial mesh instead uses the inertial extrinsics and $\mathbf s^I$. Applying both transformations to the mesh would count its rotation twice. The look-at convention reduces the output dimension, but restricts this configuration to body-centered views rather than independently targeted close-up images.

\subsubsection{Geometric validation and gain supervision}
\label{subsubsec:planner-visibility}

The observing context permits phase-dependent predictions, while explicit geometric checks determine whether the decoded images can constrain the surface. Transform the local normal of Section~\ref{subsec:spc-model-uncertainty} into the common body frame and denote it by $\mathbf n_i^B$. For each decoded observation,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mu_{ik}^{\mathrm{sun}}&=(\mathbf n_i^B)^T\mathbf s^B(\phi_k),\\
        \mathbf v_{ik}^B&=\frac{\mathbf p_k^B-\mathbf X_i^B}
                              {\|\mathbf p_k^B-\mathbf X_i^B\|},\\
        \mu_{ik}^{\mathrm{view}}&=(\mathbf n_i^B)^T\mathbf v_{ik}^B.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:planner-incidence-emission}
\end{equation}
A usable surface sample must have positive incidence and emission cosines, lie within the calibrated image frustum, and pass separate shadow and camera-occlusion tests. A positive normal--Sun dot product alone does not establish illumination on a concave body. Rays toward the Sun and camera are tested against the full mesh using a cached bounding-volume hierarchy, with surface offsets to avoid numerical self-intersections. Incidence, emission, photometric phase angle, albedo, and projected pixel scale are checked against the valid domain of the adopted reflectance and image-noise models. Grazing or unresolved observations can therefore be excluded even when geometrically visible.

An acquisition is geometrically rejected when its tested, area-weighted usable surface support falls below a configured minimum. Rejected slots reduce the accepted proposal count; they are not replaced by duplicate poses. Passing these tests establishes geometric suitability under the current mesh, not positive information gain or spacecraft reachability. The decoded positions and times must also pass an external trajectory and pointing-feasibility check before execution; minimum temporal spacing and positions outside an enclosing sphere do not establish a feasible transfer.

SCONE estimates newly covered surface using visibility and observation history~\citep{guedon2022}. Our reference score instead measures the joint covariance reduction in Equation~\ref{eq:planner-sequence-gain}. Neither visibility nor the variance-weighted illumination gate evaluates this score: repeated visible images may constrain the same modes, and a large marginal variance may remain insensitive to a proposed view.

The gain critic in Figure~\ref{fig:method-framework} requires a target matched to the images actually acquired. Let $\widetilde{\mathcal Q}$ be a decoded proposal, $\mathbf m$ its acceptance mask, and $\mathcal Q$ the acquired subsequence. The mask records geometric rejection and any further execution rejection used in that rollout. With uncertainty evaluated before acquisition and after nonlinear reconstruction, define the realized gain
\begin{equation}
    R(\mathcal Q)=\sum_i\omega_i
       \left[\sigma_{h_i,\mathrm{before}}^2
                    -\sigma_{h_i,\mathrm{after}\,\mathcal Q}^2\right].
    \label{eq:planner-realized-gain}
\end{equation}
The comparison uses the same evaluation locations, area weights, prior, reference conventions, and noise calibration. Unlike the fixed-linearization forecast $G$, $R$ may be negative after refitting. A training rollout that acquires the complete accepted sequence can supply its sequence label. If only its first acquisition is executed before replanning, the resulting change labels that singleton acquisition, not the unexecuted sequence. Training a $K>1$ sequence critic therefore requires matched sequence rollouts; single-acquisition feedback alone does not supply those labels.

For compressed pre-decision input $\mathbf x$, a gain regressor $V_\psi(\mathbf x,\widetilde{\mathcal Q},\mathbf m)$ can be fitted by
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L_{\mathrm{critic}}=
       \mathbb E_{\mathcal D}\!\left[
       \bigl(V_\psi(\mathbf x,\widetilde{\mathcal Q},\mathbf m)
                     -R(\mathcal Q)\bigr)^2\right],
    \label{eq:planner-critic-target}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal D$ contains matched reconstruction rollouts, including rejected proposals. An empty accepted sequence has zero variance gain; rejection penalties, if used, are separate from $R$. With only the compressed input, this critic estimates conditional expected realized gain over the training distribution, rather than recovering $G$ for every full SPC state. A critic intended to reproduce that reference score would instead require labels from the local information update and sufficient joint-information inputs.

The planner can use the differentiable regressor as a surrogate objective. Gradients through the illumination gate and continuous pose outputs do not differentiate the hard ray tests, rejection decisions, or SPC solver. Acceptance masks are held fixed within a local surrogate-gradient update and recomputed for each new proposal. Training must include rejected outputs and provide valid initial proposals or a separate exploration/feasibility mechanism: the zero empty-sequence target alone gives no direction out of an invalid region. This defines gain supervision; a reinforcement-learning value function would additionally require a return and update rule. Critic calibration, proposal validity, and final geometric accuracy require separate evaluation.

\subsubsection{Replanning and computation on large models}
\label{subsubsec:planner-execution}

The full-resolution SPC model is retained for reconstruction and geometric verification. Only the sampled surface enters PointNet++, keeping the neural input and intermediate tensors bounded as mesh resolution increases. The uncertainty interface can obtain the needed covariance diagonal entries through solves $\mathbf F\mathbf y_i=\mathbf e_i$, followed by $\sigma_{h_i}^2=\mathbf e_i^T\mathbf y_i$, without materializing the dense inverse. The solves retain the joint height and auxiliary state and reuse a factorization across the requested right-hand sides. They belong to the SPC uncertainty update, whose cost is separate from the forward planner evaluation.

Sampling indices, point-to-maplet associations, spatial neighborhoods, regional-feature lookup indices, illumination coefficients, and the ray-intersection hierarchy are cached while their dependencies remain unchanged. During one planning call, rotation changes directions and the illumination weights but leaves the sampled body and its encoded features unchanged. Geometry updates invalidate the affected caches; uncertainty updates require refreshing $u_i$, $\mathbf g$, $\mathbf z_i$, and the variance-weighted summaries. Simply allowing time to pass neither changes the body-frame uncertainty map nor justifies reducing its values.

Online inference gathers the current SPC state, encodes the sampled surface once, predicts $K$ phases, evaluates their illumination gates, predicts $K$ positions, and converts these outputs to extrinsics and acquisition times. For fixed feature width, the rotation and weighted-pooling branch costs $O(KM)$; the position head has an input dimension linear in the fixed budget $K$. For $M_v$ verification samples, incidence and view-direction calculations cost $O(KM_v)$, with ray-intersection cost additionally depending on the mesh and acceleration structure. The full mesh is neither rotated nor rendered for a dense hypothetical phase grid. Sampling, uncertainty estimation, and hierarchy construction remain separate costs. Verification may use denser samples near shadow boundaries and uncertain terrain, but finite sampling can still miss small features; point count and verification density require accuracy--latency evaluation.

The first geometrically accepted pair is returned as the next observation proposal, subject to the external execution check. The remaining pairs are tentative and are replaced after the next reconstruction update. If every proposal is rejected, no observation is recommended. A configured uncertainty tolerance may stop acquisition only over the evaluated surface; missing or masked regions do not thereby meet the modeling requirement.

Before acquisition, retain the current uncertainty state. After the acquired image or batch is incorporated into SPC, verify statistical-fit consistency, align the updated surface with the same evaluation locations, and compute $R$ for exactly those acquisitions. The refreshed surface and uncertainty then trigger a new plan. This closes the reconstruction--planning loop without imposing an artificial uncertainty decrease, while preserving the distinction between single-acquisition feedback and complete-sequence training labels.
