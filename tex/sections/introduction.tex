For missions to explore distant small astroids(like NASA's OSIRIS-REx\citep{alasad2021}, ESA's Rosetta\citep{taylor2017rosetta}, JAXA's Hayabusa\citep{fujiwara2006,demura2006}), reliable three-dimensional models is essential throughout multiple scientific tasks. The process of obtaining models with captured photos by equipped optical instruments generates useful quantitative measurements: they support optical navigation, provide the spatial reference for mapping, place local observations within a global geological context, support scientific interpretation and sampling operations. Stereophotoclinometry (SPC)\citep{barnouin2020} is an established approach to extract information from captured obtical images and thus derive models of astroids. It connects pixels of multiple images and illumination status to stereo constraints like elevation and albedo of a specific location, which become integratable and optimizalbe variables.Its practical use across planetary missions demonstrates its value of extrating data in roughly unknow environments with limited informations.

Because the surface topography and albedo are totally or partially unknown before reconstruction, the observation geometry is the only controllable factor that can be used to improve the quality of reconstruction, therefore the quality of modeling depends on both the reconstruction procedure and the observations made available to it. The brightness of a pixel on images depens on not only the surface topography but also the observation geometry(illumination conditions and camera extrinsic parameters). Stereo observations of multiple images constrain the three-dimensional positions of the surface, while photoclinometry estimates local slopes and relative albedo from image brightness; these slopes are integrated into surface elevations with geometric and topographic constraints. Repeatedly imaging a specific area with the same illumination condition may therefore increase its nominal coverage without adequately constraining its elevation\citep{gaskell2023,palmer2022}, which makes observation of low infomation density. This limitation makes the choice of images a scientific determinant of model quality: an observation is valuable insofar as it adds constraints that the existing image set lacks. All above makes observation planning, the outcome of a sequence of operational decisions, an important scientific task for SPC model reconstruction.

SPC modeling proceeds through successive stages of refinement, starting from an initial global shape and progressively developing more detailed local terrain(called maplets). As images with closer spatial distance become available, the existing solution supports the construction of maplets with higher resolution, allowing finer surface structure to be established \citep{barnouin2020,palmer2022}. For the model of current stage, effective planning must answer three linked questions: \emph{Which observations are still missing, how much improvement could they provide, and how can that improvement be verified?} Existing SPC studies recommend complementary viewing directions and illumination conditions, including combinations of images that emphasize topography and relative albedo \citep{palmer2022,palmer2024geometry}. These recommendations provide valuable guidance for designing an observation plan, but a hand-made geometric configuration does not meet requirements of the reconstruction perfectly or identify which observation would address the deficiencies of a particular region, which requires the observation planning and the model-quality assessment to be considered together. Previous validation studies already provide spatial diagnostics, including maplet residuals and vertex height dispersion, that help identify regions with weak or inconsistent reconstruction support \citep{alasad2021,ernst2023}. For the modeling objective considered here, that value can be expressed as the expected reduction in regional surface-height error or the increased likelihood of satisfying a prescribed accuracy tolerance, conditional on the current model and previously acquired images. Coverage and geometric diversity provide useful evidence, but their relationship to these accuracy gains is relatively weak. Existing studies\citet{piccinin2022,wang2025,weirich2022} have explored several approaches to automate observation planning, offering benefits for image selection and resource allocation. These studies demonstrate how automated planning can select useful observation combinations and account for observation costs. However, their reconstruction-related objectives remain based on geometric proxies derived from observation-angle recommendations in the SPC literature, supplemented by coverage and observation counts. These quantities characterize the suitability of the input images, but do not directly measure the geometric accuracy achieved by SPC. The distinction matters because internal consistency and actual reconstruction error require separate assessment, as demonstrated by synthetic-asteroid validation \citep{weirich2022}. Neither planning framework uses the quality diagnostics of a newly reconstructed SPC model to guide the next observation round. Consequently, satisfying its geometric criteria does not establish whether the resulting terrain meets a prescribed accuracy tolerance or which remaining deficiencies warrant further observation. A closed loop linking \textit{planning $\rightarrow$ SPC reconstruction $\rightarrow$ quantitative model evaluation $\rightarrow$ next round planning} would address this gap with an observation metric that predicts the scientific value of a candidate observation before its image is available. 

The diversity of small bodies makes generalizable observation planning necessary for extending reliable SPC reconstruction across targets. Differences in global shape, rotation, and illumination change both the availability of useful observations and the combinations needed to constrain the surface \citep{palmer2022}, which generates a substantive problem: the planner must adjust itself with the target and its observable terrain changed. A generalizable method should therefore use a common decision process to adapt strategy for reconstruction needs, and identify regions whose requirements remain unmet. Existing planning research already provides part of this evidence, with some methods demonstrating cross-target applicability and others incorporating shape-dependent visibility, illumination, and resource constraints \citep{piccinin2022,wang2025}. However, the reported effectiveness of these representative planners is evaluated through geometric mapping scores, coverage, and observation costs, without reconstructing SPC terrain models from the selected images to measure the resulting surface errors. Such evaluations establish how efficiently a plan meets its prescribed criteria, but leave uncertain how the reconstruction process benefits from those gains. Reconstruction and validation studies provide complementary evidence by producing actual SPC models and assessing their quality using internal diagnostics, independent measurements, or known synthetic topography \citep{alasad2021,weirich2022,ernst2023}. These results demonstrate what particular image collections and reconstruction procedures can achieve, yet they do not systematically test a common planning method across different targets through the accuracy of its resulting models. Therefore the unresolved issue is the connection between generalizable observation planning and verified reconstruction performance, establishing which requires following the consequences of a plan through image observation and SPC reconstruction to regional model assessment, so that improvements in surface accuracy and the extent of adequately constrained terrain can be quantified and compared across targets. 

Real mission process illustrates the image collections used for SPC reconstruction are massive. Rosetta's SPC SHAP5 model of comet 67P incorporated more than 5500 narrow-angle camera images acquired through June 2015 \citep{preusker2017}. For OSIRIS-REx, successive Bennu models v10, v20, and v42 used 2351, 5114, and 15,325 images, respectively, with product ground sample distances of 1.5 m, 75 cm, and 35 cm \citep{palmer2022}. To obtain reliable SPC inputs, observation campaigns incorporate repeated passes over target astroid and overlapping imaging sequences, creating substantial redundancy in surface coverage. During Bennu Approach, for example, position and pointing uncertainties required $2\times2$, $3\times3$, and $4\times4$ image mosaics to ensure target coverage; the subsequent Baseball Diamond campaign comprised seven passes intended to supply complementary observations for terrain reconstruction \citep{palmer2022}. This redundancy provides operational margin, but further images with similar observation geometries can yield diminishing improvements while continuing to consume observation time, image storage, downlink capacity, and human processing effort. Planning simulations also indicates avoidable over-observation: in one 67P scenario, a learned policy acquired 285 images instead of the 500 used by uniform scheduling, a 43\% reduction, while improving the geometric mapping score \citep{piccinin2022}. Acquiring additional images without assessing their contribution can therefore lead to inefficient image use and wasted observing resources, motivating a planner that directs further observations toward unresolved terrain and stops when the required model quality has been achieved.

To address the scientific question of how to acquire image combinations that satisfy the requirements of SPC modeling, this paper develops an observation planning method based on reinforcement learning. First, we establish a closed loop of \emph{planning $\rightarrow$ SPC reconstruction $\rightarrow$ accuracy assessment $\rightarrow$ replanning $\rightarrow$ and reconstruction refinement}. Quantitative evaluation indicators of regional surface errors and the extent of terrain accuracy requirements are incorporated into subsequent planning decisions, allowing the policy to identify regions that remain insufficiently constrained and select observations expected to improve them. Second, the same planning framework based on reinforcement learning is applicable to small bodies with diverse global shapes and local terrain. Its performance across targets is evaluated through the final SPC models, using quantitative evaluation to assess the effectiveness of the selected image combinations. Third, reconstruction feedback directs the available image budget toward unresolved modeling needs and reduces unnecessary repeated imaging of well constrained regions. Evaluating the number of acquired images together with the accuracy achieved by the resulting models quantifies observation efficiency, while a stopping criterion based on model quality limits further imaging once the reconstruction requirements are met.
